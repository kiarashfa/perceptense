Part
A
·
what
cinema
is
—
the
foundations
The
synthesis
art
An
art
form
that
contains
all
others
—
and
adds
something
new
Cinema
is
the
youngest
of
the
major
art
forms
—
invented
in
1895
by
the
Lumière
brothers
—
and
the
most
synthetic:
it
combines
image
(painting,
photography),
movement,
time,
narrative
(literature),
sound
(music),
and
performance.
Nothing
else
can
do
all
of
these
simultaneously.
But
cinema
is
not
simply
the
sum
of
its
ingredients:
the
cut
—
the
instantaneous
jump
from
one
image
to
another
—
is
a
technique
unique
to
film,
with
no
equivalent
in
any
other
art.
Eisenstein
called
it
the
primary
unit
of
cinematic
meaning:
not
the
shot
itself
but
the
collision
between
shots.
A
film
is
built
from
photographs
(24
per
second)
but
it
is
not
photography;
from
stories
but
it
is
not
literature;
from
music
but
it
is
not
music.
It
is
something
new:
images
in
time,
experienced
collectively
in
darkness.
Control
of
attention
The
camera
tells
you
what
to
see
Unlike
theatre
or
painting,
cinema
absolutely
controls
what
the
viewer
sees
and
when.
The
close-up
of
a
hand
reaching
for
a
gun,
the
cutaway
to
a
reaction,
the
decision
to
hold
on
a
face
for
five
seconds
or
half
a
second
—
all
of
these
are
choices
that
determine
meaning.
The
director
and
editor
decide
what
exists
in
the
viewer's
consciousness
at
every
moment.
This
totalitarian
control
of
attention
is
cinema's
most
fundamental
property.
Controlled
time
The
only
art
form
that
controls
its
own
duration
A
novel
is
read
at
the
reader's
pace;
a
painting
can
be
looked
at
for
seconds
or
hours.
Cinema
runs
at
a
fixed
rate
and
the
viewer
has
no
control
over
duration.
Slow
motion
extends
time;
fast
motion
compresses
it;
the
cut
eliminates
it
entirely.
Cinema
can
show
you
ten
years
in
ten
seconds
(a
montage)
or
ten
seconds
in
ten
minutes
(Tarkovsky).
No
other
art
form
manipulates
the
experience
of
time
so
directly.
Collective
experience
Darkness,
a
crowd,
and
a
projected
light
Cinema
was
designed
for
collective
experience
in
a
darkened
room
—
unlike
literature
(private)
or
painting
(variable).
The
theatre
of
the
cinema
screening,
with
its
shared
reactions,
its
communal
darkness,
and
the
sheer
scale
of
projection,
is
part
of
the
art
form.
Watching
a
film
alone
on
a
laptop
is
a
different
experience
from
watching
it
in
a
cinema
—
not
just
in
quality
but
in
kind.
Indexical
reality
Light
actually
reflected
from
the
real
world
A
film
is
made
of
photographs:
light
physically
reflected
from
the
world
exposed
onto
a
light-sensitive
surface
(or
its
digital
equivalent).
This
gives
cinema
a
different
relationship
to
reality
than
painting
or
drawing
—
a
photograph
of
a
face
is
caused
by
that
face.
Bazin
argued
that
cinema
has
an
"ontological
realism":
even
fiction
films
contain
an
irreducible
residue
of
the
real.
CGI
complicates
this
but
does
not
eliminate
it.

Part
B
·
film
language
—
the
grammar
of
cinema
Why
"grammar"
is
the
right
word
Film
has
rules
—
but
they
can
be
broken
deliberately
Every
film
in
the
dominant
Hollywood
tradition
is
made
according
to
a
set
of
conventions
so
thoroughly
internalised
that
audiences
never
notice
them:
the
180-degree
rule
keeps
characters
on
consistent
sides
of
the
frame
across
cuts;
eyeline
matching
makes
two
separate
shots
feel
like
two
people
looking
at
each
other;
the
establishing-shot
to
medium-shot
to
close-up
hierarchy
orients
the
viewer
in
space.
These
rules
are
the
grammar
of
invisible
editing,
articulated
by
filmmakers
like
D.W.
Griffith
between
1910
and
1920
and
refined
throughout
the
studio
era.
The
rules
matter
because
understanding
them
is
the
only
way
to
understand
why
breaking
them
—
as
Godard,
Kubrick,
or
Tarkovsky
do
—
creates
such
a
specific
effect.
A
jump
cut
is
meaningless
unless
you
know
it
violates
the
grammar.
Editing
—
the
art
of
the
cut
Camera
movement
—
seven
ways
to
move
through
space

—
everything
in

frame

Key:
hard
light,
45°
above/left

soft,

Three-point
lighting
was
standardised
in
Hollywood's
Golden
Age
(1930s-1950s).
The
key
light
is
typically
positioned
at
45°
above
and
to
one
side,
the
fill
light
reduces
contrast
on
the
shadowed
side,
and
the
back/rim
light
prevents
the
subject
merging
with
the
background.
Adjusting
the
ratio
between
key
and
fill
changes
the
mood:
a
2:1
ratio
reads
as
natural
and
warm;
8:1
reads
as
noir
and
sinister.

Part

history
&

how
cinema

Why
movements
matter
Each
movement
solved
a
problem
the
previous
one
could
not
Cinema's
history
is
not
a
simple
progression
from
primitive
to
sophisticated.
Each
major
movement
arose
in
response
to
specific
conditions
—
technological,
political,
economic,
aesthetic
—
and
posed
distinct
challenges
to
the
prevailing
orthodoxy.
Italian
Neorealism
emerged
from
wartime
rubble
and
the
bankruptcy
of
Fascist
spectacle
cinema;
it
responded
with
non-actors,
location
shooting,
and
stories
about
ordinary
poverty.
The
French
New
Wave
emerged
from
a
generation
who
had
consumed
thousands
of
films
at
the
Cinémathèque
Française;
they
responded
to
Hollywood
classicism
with
jump
cuts,
direct
address,
and
a
new
relationship
between
filmmaker
and
camera.
New
Hollywood
emerged
from
the
collapse
of
the
studio
system
and
the
arrival
of
a
film-school
generation
who
had
studied
the
Europeans.
Understanding
the
conditions
that
produced
each
movement
gives
you
the
vocabulary
to
read
the
films.

Genre

Every

to
the
audience
—

for

filmmaker
Genre
is
not
merely
a
marketing
category
—
it

a
set
of
conventions
that
the
audience
recognises
and
expects,
which
the
filmmaker
can
then
fulfil,
subvert,
or
comment
upon.
A
Western
is
not
simply
a
film
set
in
the
American
West;
it
is
a
film
that
engages
with
a

iconography
(hats,

set
of
narrative
patterns
(the

the

and
a
set
of
moral
coordinates
(civilization
vs
wilderness,
law
vs
violence).
When
Leone
made

Upon
a
Time
in
the
West
(1968),
he
made
a
film
saturated
in
Western
conventions
specifically
in
order
to
expose
and
interrogate
them.
Genre
knowledge
is

precondition
for

Part

What

a
director
an
auteur
Consistency
of
vision
across
multiple
films
—
including
the
bad
ones
Auteur
theory,
as
developed
by
the
Cahiers
du
Cinéma
critics
in
the
1950s
(Truffaut,
Godard,
Rivette,
Chabrol,
Rohmer),
proposed
that
a
director's
body
of
work
should
be
read
as
a
coherent
artistic
statement,
the
way
you
read
a
novelist's
novels.
The
test
is
not
whether
each
film
is
a
masterpiece
—
it
is
whether
the
same
preoccupations,
visual
strategies,
and
moral
universe
appear
across
the
entire
body
of
work.
Bergman's
interrogation
of
faith
and
silence;
Kubrick's
exploration
of
institutional
violence
and
human
inadequacy;
Tarkovsky's
meditation
on
time,
memory,
and
spiritual
longing:
these
are
not
accidental
but
structural.
Even
their
less-successful
films
reveal
the
same
fingerprints.
An
auteur
is
a
director
whose
personality
is
so
strong
it
leaves
traces
through
the
machinery
of
commercial
filmmaking.

what

Part

·
Q&A
1.
Is
cinema
art
or
entertainment?
Can
it
be
both?
show
answer
The
distinction
is
partly
false.
The
Godfather
is
simultaneously
one
of
the
most
critically
acclaimed
films
in
history
and
one
of
the
most
entertaining.
Hitchcock
made
enormously
popular
thrillers
that
are
also
formally
sophisticated
works
of
cinematic
art.
The
"art
vs
entertainment"
binary
tends
to
map
onto
"European
art
cinema
vs
Hollywood
commercial
cinema"
—
a
geographic
and
economic
distinction
that
is
real
but
does
not
map
cleanly
onto
quality.
The
more
useful
question
is:
does
this
film
do
what
it
is
trying
to
do
with
intelligence
and
intention?
A
brilliant
genre
film
can
achieve
more
than
a
pretentious
"serious"
film.
2.
What
is
the
auteur
theory
and
why
is
it
controversial?
show
answer
The
auteur
theory
(coined
by
French
critic
François
Truffaut
in
1954)
holds
that
the
director
is
the
primary
author
of
a
film,
in
the
same
way
a
novelist
is
the
author
of
a
book
—
and
that
the
best
directors
maintain
consistent
themes,
styles,
and
preoccupations
across
their
work
that
constitute
a
personal
vision.
It
is
controversial
because
filmmaking
is
intensely
collaborative:
the
screenwriter,
director
of
photography,
editor,
and
actors
all
shape
the
final
film
profoundly.
But
the
theory
is
useful
as
a
critical
tool:
it
gives
you
a
framework
for
comparing
films
and
tracking
the
development
of
a
vision.
It
becomes
problematic
when
it
is
used
to
erase
the
contributions
of
collaborators.
3.

do
old
films
look
"wrong"
to
modern
eyes?
show
answer
Because
film
language
is
learned,
not
natural.
Modern
viewers
have
been
trained
by
thousands
of
hours
of
contemporary
cinema
to
expect
certain
cutting
speeds,
camera
distances,
and
performance
styles.
Early
sound-era
films
typically
had
slower
editing,
more
theatrical
staging,
and
acting
styles
descended
from
stage
performance.
Silent
film
acting,
which
looks
"exaggerated"
to
modern
eyes,
was
calibrated
for
an
audience
that
could
not
hear
dialogue.
What
reads
as
wrong
is
actually
a
different,
earlier
grammar
—
fluent
and
natural
to
its
original
audiences.
4.
What
is
the
180-degree
rule
and
why
does
violating
it
feel
so
disorienting?
show
answer
The
180-degree
rule
states
that
if
two
characters
face
each
other,
the
camera
should
stay
on
one
side
of
an
imaginary
axis
between
them
across
all
shots
in
that
scene.
This
preserves
spatial
consistency:
character
A
is
always
on
the
left,
character
B
on
the
right.
"Crossing
the
line"
flips
their
positions,
which
briefly
makes
the
viewer
feel
that
the
characters
have
swapped
sides
of
the
room
—
a
deeply
unsettling
spatial
confusion.
Directors
like
Kubrick
(The
Shining)
cross
the
line
deliberately
to
induce
unease.
It
is
not
a
physical
law;
it
is
a
convention
trained
into
audiences
over
more
than
a
century
of
Hollywood
filmmaking.
5.
What
is
the
difference
between
a
cut
and
a
dissolve,
and
when
do
you
use
each?
show
answer
A
cut
is
instantaneous
—
the
frame
changes
with
no
overlap.
A
dissolve
(or
cross-dissolve)
blends
two
images:
the
outgoing
image
fades
out
as
the
incoming
image
fades
in.
Cuts
signal
"this
is
happening
in
the
same
time
and
space"
or
create
simple
juxtaposition.
Dissolves
signal
time
passing
or
a
softer,
more
lyrical
transition
—
they
were
almost
the
default
transition
in
the
Hollywood
studio
era.
Contemporary
filmmakers
avoid
dissolves
because
they
read
as
old-fashioned
or
sentimental,
though
directors
like
Terrence
Malick
(The
Tree
of
Life)
use
them
precisely
for
that
associative,
memory-like
quality.
The
choice
between
them
is
a
statement
about
time,
causality,
and
tone.
6.
Did
the
French
New
Wave
actually
invent
the
jump
cut?
show
answer
Technically,
no
—
jump
cuts
appeared
in
early
cinema
before
grammar
was
even
standardised.
But
Godard's
use
in
À
bout
de
souffle
(Breathless,
1960)
was
the
first
time
a
jump
cut
was
used
consciously
as
a
statement
within
an
otherwise
narrative
film,
and
it
felt
like
a
violation
because
the
grammar
had
by
then
been
so
thoroughly
codified.
Godard
was
aware
of
the
rules
he
was
breaking.
His
jumps
cut
out
seconds
from
a
single
take
of
a
person
in
a
moving
car
—
they
were
also
partly
a
necessity
(cutting
down
long
takes),
but
the
effect
became
aestheticised,
defining
a
new
visual
vocabulary
that
made
"wrong"
feel
intentional
and
even
correct.
7.
What
ended
New
Hollywood?
show
answer
The
conventional
answer
is
Jaws
(1975)
and
Star
Wars
(1977),
which
demonstrated
that
a
single
blockbuster
could
generate
more
revenue
than
dozens
of
"personal"
films
and
shifted
studio
investment
toward
event
movies.
But
the
internal
explanation
is
also
important:
many
New
Hollywood
directors
grew
too
expensive
and
unreliable
(Cimino's
Heaven's
Gate
cost
$44
million
in
1980
and
destroyed
United
Artists),
while
others
like
Spielberg
and
Lucas
turned
out
to
be
perfectly
comfortable
making
blockbusters.
The
movement
didn't
end
so
much
as
split
—
some
directors
went
independent,
some
made
blockbusters,
and
the
studios
reorganised
around
a
few
tentpole
releases
per
year.
8.
Is
"genre"
purely
about
commercial
classification,
or
does
it
have
aesthetic
meaning?
show
answer
Genre
has
deep
aesthetic
meaning
—
it
functions
like
a
musical
key
or
a
poetic
form.
The
Western's
iconography
of
wide
open
space,
the
noir's
rain-slicked
streets
and
femme
fatale,
the
horror
film's
manipulation
of
sound
and
darkness:
these
are
not
just
marketing
signals
but
a
set
of
formal
tools
with
accumulated
emotional
meanings.
Rick
Altman's
influential
genre
theory
describes
genres
as
both
"semantic"
(surface
elements:
costumes,
settings)
and
"syntactic"
(underlying
structures:
narrative
patterns,
moral
logic).
The
most
interesting
genre
films
tend
to
be
those
that
interrogate
the
syntax
while
preserving
the
semantics
—
they
look
like
genre
but
feel
like
something
else.
Chinatown
(1974)
looks
like
noir
and
systematically
dismantles
noir's
moral
universe.
9.
Why
is
horror
one
of
the
most
critically
and
commercially
durable
genres?
show
answer
Horror
is
cheap
to
make
and
reliable
at
the
box
office
—
low
budgets
mean
that
even
modest
grosses
are
profitable,
which
is
why
studios
continue
to
fund
it
even
when
critics
dismiss
it.
But
its
durability
also
reflects
deeper
functions:
horror
externalises
social
anxiety.
Every
era's
horror
films
encode
its
specific
fears.
1950s
American
horror
was
saturated
with
radiation
and
invasion
metaphors
(The
Thing
from
Another
World,
Invasion
of
the
Body
Snatchers).
1970s
horror
(The
Texas
Chain
Saw
Massacre,
Halloween)
expressed
anxieties
about
family,
suburban
safety,
and
the
aftermath
of
Vietnam.
Jordan
Peele's
Get
Out
(2017)
used
horror
vocabulary
to
make
a
film
about
racism
that
could
not
have
been
made
as
a
drama
with
the
same
impact.
Horror's
formal
license
to
go
somewhere
unbearably
uncomfortable
is
precisely
what
makes
it
useful.
10.
Does
auteur
theory
erase
the
contribution
of
screenwriters?
show
answer
Yes,
to
a
significant
degree
—
and
this
is
the
most
legitimate
criticism
of
auteur
theory.
Herman
J.
Mankiewicz
co-wrote
Citizen
Kane
with
Orson
Welles;
Robert
Towne
wrote
Chinatown;
Julius
Epstein,
Philip
Epstein,
and
Howard
Koch
wrote
Casablanca.
In
each
case,
the
director
gets
the
auteur
credit
while
the
writers
are
comparatively
invisible.
Pauline
Kael's
1971
essay
"Raising
Kane"
specifically
attacked
the
auteur-isation
of
Citizen
Kane
at
Mankiewicz's
expense.
The
reality
is
that
many
great
films
are
great
collaborations,
and
auteur
theory,
while
a
useful
critical
lens,
systematically
undervalues
the
screenplay.
The
best
response
is
to
use
it
as
one
tool
among
several,
not
as
the
complete
account
of
how
films
get
made.
11.
Can
a
director
who
mainly
makes

films
be
an
auteur?
show
answer
Absolutely,
and
this
was
part
of
the
original
Cahiers
argument:
they
were
specifically
interested
in
Hollywood
directors
—
Hawks,
Ford,
Hitchcock,
Fuller,
Sirk
—
who
were
working
within
studio
genre
constraints
but
whose
personal
visions
were
visible
despite
(or
because
of)
those
constraints.
Hitchcock
is
the
clearest
case:
he
made
commercial
thrillers
with
stars
and
studio
budgets,
but
the
films
are
unmistakably
his
—
the
wrong
man,
the
controlling
relationship,
the
staircase,
the
blonde.
Howard
Hawks's
Westerns,
screwball
comedies,
and
noirs
all
share
a

world
view
(professional

as

self-possessed
women)
that
transcends

The
studio
constraint

opposed

12.
Why
does
South
Korean
cinema
punch
so
far
above
its
weight

show
answer
Several
factors
converged.
South
Korea
has
a
highly
educated,
cinema-going
public
and
a
domestic

that
received
significant
government
protection
and
subsidy
in
the
1990s
(a
law
requiring
Korean
films
to
fill
a
minimum
percentage
of
screen
time).
This
created
a
protected
market
in
which
Korean
directors
could
develop
genre
skills
—
particularly
in
crime,
thriller,
and
dark
comedy
—
to
a
high
level.
Directors
like
Park
Chan-wook
(Oldboy),
Bong
Joon-ho
(Memories
of
Murder,
Parasite),
and
Lee
Chang-dong
(Poetry,
Burning)
combine

intelligence
with

in
a
way
that
appealed

and

Parasite

2020

Picture
Oscar

13.
What
is
the
significance
of
the
Iranian
New
Wave
—
and
can

be
made

show
answer
Iranian
cinema
since
the
1979
Revolution
has
operated
under
strict
censorship
—
women
must
wear
headscarves
on
screen,
physical
contact
between
unrelated
men
and
women
is
prohibited,
and
content
critical
of
the
Islamic
Republic
is
forbidden.
Rather
than
killing
the
art
form,
these
constraints
produced
a
distinctive
aesthetic:
directors
like
Abbas
Kiarostami,
Jafar
Panahi,
and
Mohsen
Makhmalbaf
found
indirect,
metaphorical,
and
formally
inventive
ways
to
explore
Iranian
society
within
(and
sometimes
by
deliberately
violating)
the
rules.
Kiarostami's
Close-Up
(1990)
is
a
masterpiece
of
blurred
documentary
and
fiction.
Panahi
has
made
three
films
after
being
banned
from
filmmaking
—
This
Is
Not
a
Film
(2011),
shot
secretly
on
an
iPhone.
The
Iranian
case
is
the
strongest
evidence
that
censorship
does
not
prevent
art;
it
redirects
it.

Do

need

watch
films

show
answer
For
foreign-language
films:
yes,
always
with
subtitles,

you
want
to
experience
the
film
as
it
was
made.
The
voice
performance
—
its
rhythm,
texture,
emotional
pitch
—
is
as
much
part
of
the
film
as
the
image.
Dubbing
replaces
the
original
performance

a

one,
usually
inferior
and
always
calibrated
for
synchronisation
rather
than
emotional
truth.
The
claim
that
subtitles
are
distracting
is
accurate
for
the
first
few
foreign
films
you
watch;
after
a
dozen,
your
eye
and
brain
learn
to
process
them
alongside
the
image
without
conscious
effort.
The
adjustment
takes
perhaps
four
or
five
films
—
a
small
investment
for
access
to
almost
the
entirety
of
world
cinema.

—

—

show

Context
enriches

Watch

about

The
first
watch

to

experience

—
your

responses

data.
The
second
watch,

with

The
exception
is

that
depend
on

or

cannot

—

Story
(1953)

no

and

post-war
generation
gap

a
different,
thinner
experience
than
watching
it
informed.
But
for
most
films,
prior
context
is
less
important
than
sustained
attention
during
the
film
itself.
16.
What
is
the
most
useful
habit
for
developing
film
literacy
quickly?
show
answer
The
single
most
effective
habit
is
to
pause

film

notice
a
strong
emotional
response
—
fear,
sadness,

discomfort
—

ask

specific
technical
choices
caused
it.
Was
it
a
cut?
A
piece
of
music
entering
or
exiting?
A
close-up?
A
camera
movement?
This
reverses
the
usual
experience:
instead
of
being
passively
moved,
you
become
an
active
analyst
in
real
time.
Within
ten
or
fifteen
films
watched
this
way,
the
grammar
of
cinema
becomes
transparent.
Roger
Ebert
made
the
related
point
that
what
matters
in
a
film
is
not
what
it
is
about,
but
how
it
is
about
it
—
the
"how"
is
the
film
language,
and
learning
to
read
it
is
the
whole
project
of
film
literacy.
