Overview
Sport
is
one
of
the
oldest
and
most
universal
human
activities,
yet
it
is
also
surprisingly
hard
to
define.
At
its
core,
sport
combines
physical
skill,
rule-bound
competition,
and
a
social
audience
—
distinguishing
it
from
mere
play
(no
rules,
no
audience)
and
from
games
like
chess
(no
physical
skill
threshold).
This
module
builds
a
complete
mental
model
of
sport:
why
humans
play,
how
bodies
and
minds
perform,
who
controls
the
money,
and
how
the
world's
most
popular
game
—
association
football
—
works
at
every
level
from
kick-off
to
contract
clause.
Why
humans
play
sport
Sport
solves
several
problems
at
once.
Evolutionarily,
it
is
honest
signalling:
costly
physical
performance
that
cannot
easily
be
faked,
advertising
genetic
quality,
coordination
ability,
and
pain
tolerance
to
potential
mates
and
allies.
Johan
Huizinga's
1938
concept
of
"Homo
Ludens"
argues
that
play
is
not
a
byproduct
of
survival
but
constitutive
of
culture
—
that
meaningful
human
civilization
requires
it.
Sport
also
generates
flow
states
—
Mihaly
Csikszentmihalyi's
term
for
the
absorption
that
occurs
when
challenge
precisely
matches
skill.
The
brain
releases
dopamine,
noradrenaline,
and
anandamide
during
intense
play,
making
winning
biologically
rewarding.
Sociologically,
sport
creates
in-group
cohesion
and
shared
identity.
Henri
Tajfel's
social
identity
theory
explains
why
fans
experience
their
team's
win
as
a
personal
victory:
group
membership
is
internalized
as
self-concept.
Roughly
70%
of
British
men
report
that
following
sport
is
important
to
their
sense
of
identity
(YouGov,
2019).
This
attachment
is
not
irrational;
it
generates
genuine
community,
ritual,
and
belonging
that
modern
life
often
fails
to
provide
elsewhere.
Counterintuitive
fact:
sport
watching
produces
measurable
testosterone
spikes
in
fans
of
the
winning
team
—
even
when
they
did
nothing
physical.
A
1998
study
found
male
Brazil
fans'
testosterone
rose
~28%
after
their
team
won
a
World
Cup
match,
and
dropped
a
similar
amount
after
a
loss.
Identity
and
biology
are
inseparable
here.
Sport
vs
games
vs
play
—
where
the
lines
fall
The
philosopher
Bernard
Suits
defined
sport
as
an
activity
requiring
physical
skill,
rules,
and
an
institutional
structure
(governing
body,
ranked
competition).
By
this
definition,
chess
is
a
game
but
not
a
sport;
jogging
is
physical
recreation
but
not
sport;
poker
is
a
game.
The
boundary
matters
practically:
the
IOC
debates
whether
eSports
and
chess
deserve
Olympic
status
on
exactly
these
grounds.
A
key
property
of
sport
is
the
constitutive
rule
:
rules
that
don't
just
regulate
an
activity
but
create
it.
The
offside
rule
doesn't
restrict
football
—
it
creates
football
as
we
know
it.
This
is
why
sports
feel
so
different
from
work:
the
voluntary
acceptance
of
unnecessary
constraints
is
what
makes
the
achievement
meaningful.
Usain
Bolt
running
100
metres
is
meaningless
unless
everyone
agrees
to
start
at
the
same
line
and
not
take
a
bus.
The
history
of
organised
sport
The
ancient
Olympics
began
in
776
BCE
at
Olympia,
Greece,
as
a
religious
festival
honouring
Zeus.
Events
included
the
stadion
foot-race
(roughly
192
metres),
wrestling,
discus,
javelin,
and
chariot
racing.
Competitors
were
Greek
citizens
who
trained
full-time,
often
sponsored
by
wealthy
patrons
—
not
amateur
idealists.
The
games
were
abolished
by
Emperor
Theodosius
I
in
393
CE
as
pagan
practice.
Modern
codified
sport
emerged
in
19th-century
Britain,
where
industrialisation
compressed
work
and
leisure
into
distinct
spheres,
and
railway
networks
allowed
teams
to
travel.
The
Football
Association
was
founded
in
1863.
The
Marquess
of
Queensberry
Rules
for
boxing
appeared
in
1867.
Wimbledon
launched
in
1877.
This
codification
wave
was
also
a
class
project:
elite
schools
(Eton,
Rugby)
formalized
games
that
had
previously
been
unruly
mob
activities.
Television
transformed
sport
into
a
global
product.
The
1936
Berlin
Olympics
were
the
first
televised
games
(to
28
closed-circuit
venues).
By
1996,
Atlanta
attracted
3.5
billion
TV
viewers.
Today,
the
Super
Bowl
generates
~$600
million
in
US
ad
revenue
in
a
single
evening,
and
the
FIFA
World
Cup
final
is
reliably
the
most
watched
single
broadcast
event
on
Earth,
drawing
over
1.5
billion
viewers
in
2022.
The
world's
most

of
"global

combine
active
participation
and

Cricket's
following
is

in

football

have

Part

the

of
sport
—

Human
physical
limits
—
what
the
body
can
and
cannot
do
VO₂
max
—
the
maximum
rate
of
oxygen
consumption
—
is
the
single
best
predictor
of
endurance
performance.
Average
untrained
adults
score
35–45
ml/kg/min.
Elite
cyclists
like
Oskar
Syklebust
have
recorded
values
above
97.
The
practical
ceiling
may
be
around
100,
dictated
by
cardiac
output
and
the
diffusion
capacity
of
the
lungs.
Eliud
Kipchoge,
who
ran
the
first
sub-2-hour
marathon
in
2019,
has
a
VO₂
max
of
~92.
Muscle
fibres
determine
athletic
specialisation.
Type
I
(slow-twitch)
fibres
use
oxygen
efficiently
and
resist
fatigue
—
marathoners
have
roughly
80%
Type
I.
Type
II
(fast-twitch)
fibres
contract
explosively
but
tire
quickly
—
sprinters
may
have
75%
Type
II.
This
ratio
is
~50%
genetic,
making
a
true
sprinter
becoming
a
world-class
marathon
runner
essentially
impossible
at
elite
level.
The
100-metre
sprint
ceiling:
biomechanical
models
suggest
the
absolute
human
limit
is
approximately
9.48
seconds,
versus
Usain
Bolt's
9.58
in
2009.
The
constraint
is
not
just
muscle
speed
but
ground
contact
time
—
below
about
80
milliseconds
per
step,
the
foot
cannot
generate
enough
force.

Training
principles
—
how
elite
athletes
improve
Progressive
overload
is
the
foundational
principle:
the
training
stimulus
must
continually
exceed
the
body's
current
capacity
to
produce
adaptation.
Rest
is
not
recovery
from
failure
—
it
is
when
the
adaptation
actually
occurs
(supercompensation).
This
is
why
overtraining
is
a
real
pathology:
athletes
who
never
allow
recovery
plateau
or
regress.
Periodisation
structures
training
across
cycles:
a
macrocycle
(full
season
or
year),
mesocycles
(4–6
weeks
each
with
different
emphases),
and
microcycles
(weekly
training
blocks).
The
Soviet
sports
scientist
Lev
Matveyev
formalized
this
in
the
1960s,
and
it
remains
the
backbone
of
elite
preparation.
Modern
variants
include
"block
periodisation"
used
by
Norwegian
swimmer
Henrik
Christiansen:
intense
focus
on
one
physical
quality
at
a
time
rather
than
concurrent
development.
The
"10,000
hours"
rule
from
Malcolm
Gladwell
(drawn
from
Anders
Ericsson's
research)
is
widely
misunderstood.
Ericsson's
actual
finding
was
that
deliberate
practice
—
not
passive
repetition
—
drives
expertise,
and
the
10,000
figure
was
an
average,
not
a
threshold.
Some
elite
tennis
players
(Roger
Federer,
who
had
a
late
start
in
tennis
specifically)
accumulated
far
fewer
hours.
Genetics,
coaching
quality,
and
early
specialisation
age
all
interact
with
practice
volume.

Sports
psychology
—
concentration,
pressure,
and
the
mental
game
Choking
occurs
when
performance
degrades
under
pressure
despite
sufficient
skill.
The
mechanism
is
well
understood:
anxiety
redirects
conscious
attention
toward
the
execution
of
actions
normally
automated.
Jana
Sackmeister
and
colleagues
demonstrated
in
2010
that
expert
golfers
putt
worse
when
told
to
focus
on
"keeping
the
left
arm
straight"
—
a
cue
novices
benefit
from
but
experts
don't,
because
their
swing
is
already
implicit.
This
is
why
commentary
about
"thinking
too
much"
is
neurologically
accurate.
Flow
state
—
Csikszentmihalyi's
concept
of
total
absorption
—
correlates
with
a
specific
EEG
pattern:
increased
alpha
waves
in
the
left
temporal
lobe,
associated
with
reduced
self-monitoring.
Athletes
describe
it
as
effortless
control,
slowed
time,
and
the
disappearance
of
self-consciousness.
Conditions
for
flow:
clear
goals,
immediate
feedback,
and
challenge-skill
balance
within
roughly
4%
of
current
capacity.
Common
misconception:
positive
self-talk
("I
can
do
this")
is
always
better
than
negative
self-talk.
Research
by
Antonis
Hatzigeorgiadis
(2011)
found
instructional
self-talk
("bend
your
knees
on
contact")
outperforms
motivational
self-talk
for
skill-based
tasks.
The
right
mental
cue
depends
entirely
on
what
the
task
requires.
Nutrition
and
doping
—
optimisation
vs
cheating
Sports
nutrition
operates
on
marginal
gains.
Creatine
monohydrate
is
the
most
evidence-supported
legal
supplement:
3–5g
daily
increases
phosphocreatine
stores
in
muscle,
improving
high-intensity
performance
by
5–10%
in
trained
athletes.
Caffeine
(3–6
mg/kg
body
weight)
demonstrably
reduces
perceived
exertion
and
improves
endurance
by
2–4%
—
which
at
the
elite
level
is
the
difference
between
a
medal
and
10th
place.
Both
are
legal.
Performance-enhancing
drug
use
has
accompanied
modern
sport
since
the
1950s.
EPO
(erythropoietin)
revolutionised
cycling
in
the
1990s:
it
stimulates
red
blood
cell
production,
raising
haematocrit
and
VO₂
max
by
up
to
10%.
The
USADA
found
that
Lance
Armstrong's
US
Postal
Service
team
ran
"the
most
sophisticated,
professionalised,
and
successful
doping
programme"
in
sport
history
—
Armstrong
won
7
consecutive
Tours
de
France
between
1999–2005,
all
of
which
were
later
stripped.
WADA
(World
Anti-Doping
Agency),
established
in
1999,
maintains
the
Prohibited
List
of
~300
substances
across
categories.
The
biological
passport
—
introduced
in
2008
—
tracks
longitudinal
blood
parameters
rather
than
testing
for
specific
substances,
making
it
much
harder
to
dope
without
detection.
Russia's
state-sponsored
doping
programme
(revealed
by
whistleblower
Grigory
Rodchenkov,
2015)
involved
switching
urine
samples
through
a
hole
in
a
Sochi
lab
wall,
rendering
standard
testing
useless.

The
sports
economy
—
how
sport
makes
and
spends
money
Global
sport
is
a
$500+
billion
industry.
The
dominant
revenue
engine
is
broadcast
rights,
not
ticket
sales
or
merchandise.
The
NFL's
TV
rights
deals
(2023–2033)
total
$113
billion
across
CBS,
NBC,
Fox,
ESPN/ABC,
and
Amazon
—
approximately
$11
billion
per
year
for
one
domestic
league.
The
Premier
League
earns
~£3
billion
per
season
from
UK
broadcast
deals
alone,
plus
~£1.6
billion
from
international
rights.
Athlete
salaries
at
the
top
tier
are
extraordinary.
As
of
2024,
Cristiano
Ronaldo
earns
approximately
$200
million
per
year
total
(salary
+
commercial),
making
him
the
world's
highest-paid
athlete.
The
NBA
minimum
rookie
salary
is
$1.1
million;
the
median
is
$3.8
million.
But
these
figures
obscure
the
pyramid
structure:
the
vast
majority
of
professional
athletes
globally
earn
near
minimum
wage,
and
career
lengths
in
contact
sports
average
just
3–4
years.

Sports
governance
—
who
runs
sport
and
the
corruption
problem
Most
global
sports
are
governed
by
non-profit
bodies
with
enormous
commercial
revenues
and
minimal
external
accountability.
FIFA,
which
governs
football
globally,
had
$7.5
billion
revenue
in
the
2022
World
Cup
cycle.
The
IOC
controls
a
multi-billion-dollar
broadcast
empire
while
its
105
members
are
unelected
and
permanent.
This
structural
opacity
has
generated
systemic
corruption.
The
2015
FBI/DoJ
investigation
into
FIFA
indicted
47
individuals
and
entities
for
wire
fraud,
racketeering,
and
money
laundering
totalling
$200
million
in
bribes.
Sepp
Blatter
and
Michel
Platini
were
banned
for
8
years.
The
Salt
Lake
City
Olympics
bribery
scandal
(1998)
involved
13
IOC
members
accepting
money
or
gifts
in
exchange
for
hosting
votes.
The
structural
problem:
international
sports
federations
are
often
registered
in
Switzerland,
which
has
historically
provided
legal
shelter
from
foreign
law
enforcement.
FIFA
is
incorporated
in
Zurich.
Post-2015,
the
US
relied
on
wire
fraud
statutes
—
asserting
jurisdiction
whenever
a
US
dollar
crossed
a
US
server
—
to
prosecute
what
Swiss
law
could
not
reach.
Sport
and
politics
—
when
they
cannot
be
separated
The
1936
Berlin
Olympics
were
explicitly
designed
by
the
Nazi
regime
as
a
propaganda
showcase.
Jesse
Owens'
four
gold
medals
—
before
an
audience
that
included
Hitler
—
are
remembered
as
a
rebuke
to
Aryan
superiority
ideology.
In
fact,
Owens
himself
said
Hitler
waved
to
him
from
his
box;
it
was
President
Roosevelt
who
never
sent
Owens
a
telegram
of
congratulation.
The
politics
cut
in
multiple
directions.
Apartheid-era
South
Africa
was
expelled
from
the
IOC
in
1970
and
FIFA
in
1961.
The
international
sporting
boycott
is
widely
cited
as
one
of
the
most
effective
non-military
economic
and
reputational
pressures
applied
to
the
regime,
contributing
to
its
eventual
isolation.
Colin
Kaepernick's
NFL
kneeling
protests
(2016–)
cost
him
his
career
but
generated
a
global
conversation:
Nike's
"Believe
in
Something"
campaign
featuring
Kaepernick
reportedly
increased
Nike's
online
sales
31%
in
the
week
after
launch.
Sportswashing
—
using
sport
to
launder
a
country's
international
reputation
—
is
the
dominant
political
concern
of
the
2020s.
Saudi
Arabia's
Public
Investment
Fund
owns
Newcastle
United,
LIV
Golf,
the
Dakar
Rally,
and
has
hosted
heavyweight
boxing
world
title
fights.
Qatar
hosted
the
2022
World
Cup
despite
evidence
of
migrant
worker
deaths
during
construction.
The
argument
that
"engagement
beats
isolation"
has
merit
but
also
serves
as
a
post-hoc
justification
for
financial
self-interest
by
governing
bodies.
Analytics
and
data
—
how
sport
became
a
data
science
problem
The
Moneyball
revolution,
documented
by
Michael
Lewis
in
2003,
describes
how
the
Oakland
Athletics
under
GM
Billy
Beane
used
on-base
percentage
(OBP)
—
a
statistic
ignored
by
the
market
—
to
acquire
undervalued
players
and
compete
with
a
payroll
40%
below
the
Yankees.
The
underlying
insight
was
that
human
scouts
systematically
misprice
players
because
they
weight
observable,
emotionally
salient
qualities
(appearance,
confidence,
throwing
style)
over
predictive
statistics.
The
A's
won
103
games
in
2002
with
a
$44
million
payroll.
Football's
data
revolution
came
later.
Opta
began
collecting
event
data
in
1996;
StatsBomb
launched
player
tracking
in
2014.
Expected
goals
(xG)
—
the
probability
a
given
shot
results
in
a
goal,
based
on
location,
assist
type,
and
body
part
—
is
now
the
standard
metric.
Clubs
like
Brentford
and
Brighton,

The

Offside

A

—

This

The

Fan

is
net
negative
in

The

The

(always

to

(foul

(not

The

Modern

A

Pressing

Gegenpressing
("counter-pressing"
in

refers
to
immediately

the
ball
back
after
losing
possession,
before

opposition

Jürgen

developed

at

Dortmund
between

drawing
on
Ralf
Rangnick's
predecessor
work
at
RB
Leipzig.
The
logic:
for
5–8
seconds
after
losing
the
ball,
attacking
players
are
closer
to
the
ball
than
defenders,
creating
a
brief
window
to
win
it
back
in
a
dangerous
area.

(Passes
Allowed
Per
Defensive
Action)
is

Lower

=

pressing.
Elite

teams
like
Klopp's
Liverpool
2019–20
averaged
~6

a

averages
12–18.
The

cover
3–4
km
more
than

in

and
see

across

season.
The

Low-quality

can

(Iniesta,

with

control
and
short
combinations.
Manchester

under

as

Juego

Its

The

(as

The

—

It

Guardiola's

could

to

The

The

football
allowed

The

October
26,
1863,

Blackheath

(kicking

(founded

(whose

to

for

When

and

to

league,
the
FA
relented.
The

League
—

first

—
launched
in

12

clubs.
Aston

What

does
—
and
how
the

The
19th-century
football
manager
was
primarily
a
secretary
—
organising
fixtures,
travel,
and
facilities.
Tactical
authority
remained
with
players
(often
the
team
captain).
The
shift
to
manager-as-tactician
happened
gradually
between
1900–1950;
Herbert
Chapman
at
Arsenal
(1925–1934)
is
the
first
clearly
modern
manager,
redesigning
Chapman's
position
on
the
pitch,
introducing
the
WM
formation,
installing
floodlights,
and
even
persuading
London
Underground
to
rename
Gillespie
Road
station
"Arsenal."
The
modern
manager
operates
within
a
sporting
director
model
—
a
continental
structure
where
recruitment,
contracts,
and
squad
planning
are
handled
by
a
sporting
director,
leaving
the
manager
free
to
focus
on
coaching
and
tactics.
Liverpool's
Julian
Ward
(later
Michael
Edwards)
and
Brighton's
Paul
Barber
exemplify
this.
The
alternative
is
the
traditional
English
"manager-in-charge-of-everything"
model,
which
Ferguson
at
Manchester
United
practised
until
2013
but
which
is
increasingly
rare.
Backroom
staff
has
exploded
since
2000.
A
Premier

club's

team
now
routinely
includes:
