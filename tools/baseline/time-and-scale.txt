Gregorian
The
Gregorian
calendar
was
introduced
by
Pope
Gregory
XIII
in
1582
to
fix
a
10-day
drift
in
the
Julian
calendar.
Catholic
countries
switched
immediately;
Protestant
Britain
did
not
adopt
it
until
1752
--
by
which
point
11
days
had
to
be
dropped.
People
rioted
in
the
streets
shouting
"Give
us
back
our
11
days."
Islamic
Because
the
Hijri
year
is
11
days
shorter
than
the
solar
year,
Ramadan
shifts
through
all
seasons
in
a
cycle
of
roughly
33
years.
A
Muslim
born
in
1990
will
have
experienced
Ramadan
in
summer,
autumn,
winter,
and
spring
before
age
33.
This
is
considered
theologically
significant:
everyone
must
experience
fasting
under
both
easy
and
hard
conditions.
Persian
The
Persian
Shamsi
calendar
is
widely
considered
the
most
astronomically
accurate
calendar
in
regular
use.
Its
leap
year
rule
--
adding
a
leap
day
in
specific
patterns
over
a
2,820-year
grand
cycle
--
keeps
it
within
one
day
of
the
true
solar
year
for
that
entire
period,
compared
to
the
Gregorian
calendar's
periodic
drift.
Nowruz
(New
Year)
always
falls
on
or
within
one
day
of
the
March
equinox.
Hebrew
The
Hebrew
calendar
inserts
a
13th
"leap
month"
(Adar
II)
in
7
out
of
every
19
years
--
the
Metonic
cycle,
which
nearly
perfectly
synchronises
the
solar
and
lunar
cycles.
This
ensures
Jewish
holidays
always
fall
in
the
same
season:
Passover
is
always
in
spring,
Sukkot
in
autumn.
Chinese
The
Chinese

calendar
underlies

cycles:
the
12-year
animal
zodiac
(rat,
ox,
tiger...)
combined
with
a
10-stem
cycle
producing
a
60-year
"sexagenary
cycle."
The
calendar
also
defines
24
solar
terms
(jieqi),
which
historically
guided
agricultural
decisions.
Chinese
New
Year
falls
between

21

20

--
