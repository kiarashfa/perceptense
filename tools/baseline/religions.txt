Defining
religion:
a
harder
problem
than
it
seems
Every
definition
of
religion
either
includes
things
we
would
rather
exclude
(football
fandom
as
"religion"?)
or
excludes
things
we
would
rather
include
(is
Theravada
Buddhism
a
religion
if
it
has
no
god?).
Scholars
distinguish
substantive
definitions,
religion
involves
belief
in
the
supernatural,
from
functional
ones:
religion
does
what
religion
does,
binding
communities,
answering
ultimate
questions,
and
marking
the
sacred.
Neither
works
cleanly.
Anthropologist
Clifford
Geertz
proposed
that
a
religion
is
"a
system
of
symbols
which
acts
to
establish
powerful,
pervasive,
and
long-lasting
moods
and
motivations."
That
captures
something
real,
but
it
would
also
describe
Maoism.
The
messiness
is
not
a
flaw;
it
reflects
the
fact
that
"religion"
is
a
Western
category
that
may
not
carve
nature
at
its
joints.
Many
traditions
(Shinto,
Confucianism,
many
Indigenous
practices)
do
not
have
a
word
for
"religion"
at
all.

Cognitive
science

(CSR)
asks
not
whether
any
religion
is
true
but
why
religion
is
universal.

Religious
studies
as
a
discipline
Religious
studies
emerged
in
the
19th
century
as
scholars
tried
to
study
religion
empirically
and
comparatively,
without
either
defending
or
attacking
any
tradition.
Emile
Durkheim
(1912)
argued
religion
is
essentially
social:
the
"sacred"
marks
off
what
a
community
holds
collectively
important,
and
ritual
reinforces
group
cohesion.
Max
Weber's
Protestant
Ethic
(1905)
traced
a
line
from
Calvinist
theology
to
capitalist
discipline.
William
James
(1902)
took
phenomenology
seriously,
asking
what
religious
experience
actually
feels
like
from
the
inside.
These
three
still
define
the
main
approaches:
structuralist-sociological
(Durkheim),
historical-comparative
(Weber),
and
experiential-phenomenological
(James).
Modern
scholars
add
cognitive
science
and
neuroscience,
giving
us
perhaps
the
best
tools
yet
for
understanding
religion
without
either
worshipping
or
dismissing
it.
Sacred
texts:
how
scriptures
work
and
how
they're
interpreted
Most
religions
have
texts
they
treat
as
authoritative,
but
"authoritative"
means
different
things.
For
Muslims,
the
Quran
is
literally
the
word
of
God
dictated
to
Muhammad:
every
Arabic
word
matters.
For
Jews,
the
Torah
is
also
revealed,
but
the
Talmud
(rabbinic
commentary
developed
over
centuries)
has
equal
practical
authority
in
most
denominations.
For
Christians,
the
Bible
is
"inspired",
but
exactly
how
that
word
is
interpreted
separates
the
fundamentalist
who
reads
Genesis
literally
from
the
liberal
Protestant
who
treats
it
as
theological
poetry.
Hinduism
has
no
single
canon
at
all:
the
Vedas,
Upanishads,
Bhagavad
Gita,
Puranas,
and
regional
texts
all
claim
varying
degrees
of
authority.
When
someone
says
"the
religion
teaches
X,"
always
ask:
which
interpretation,
by
whom,
in
which
tradition?

What

three
share,

All
three
traditions
trace
their
genealogy
to
Abraham
and
his
covenant
with
one
God;
all
revere
Jerusalem;
all
are
prophetic
religions
with
scriptures
that
record
God's
communication
with
humanity;
all
anticipate
some
form
of
final
judgment.
The
divergence
is
largely
about
Jesus
and
Muhammad.
Jews
do
not
accept
Jesus
as
messiah
(the
messiah
has
not
come
yet)
and
consider
Muhammad
irrelevant.
Christians
accept
Jesus
as
divine
messiah
and
son
of
God
but
do
not
accept
Muhammad's
prophethood.
Muslims
honour
Jesus
as
a
great
prophet
but
reject
his
divinity,
the
Quran
explicitly
states
God
has
no
son,
and
consider
Muhammad
the
final
and
greatest
prophet.
The
irony
is
that
the
closer
two
traditions
are
theologically,
the
sharper
their
historical
conflict:
Jewish-Christian
relations
were
far
more
violent
across
history
than,
say,
Christian-Buddhist
ones.

Hinduism:
the

oldest

religion
Hinduism
is
the
world's
third-largest
religion
(1.2
billion)
and
the
hardest
to
define,
because
it
has
no
single
founder,
no
central
authority,
no
single
scripture,
and
no
agreed-upon
creed.
Scholar
Wendy
Doniger
calls
it
"the
religion
that
has
no
name":
the
term
"Hindu"
was
coined
by
Persian
and
later
British
outsiders.
What
most
Hindu
traditions
share:
Brahman
(the
ultimate
impersonal
reality
underlying
all
existence),
Atman
(the
individual
soul
identical
with
Brahman),
Maya
(the
veil
of
illusion
that
makes
us
perceive
a
world
of
distinct
objects),
Karma
(the
ethical
law
of
cause
and
effect
across
lives),
and
Dharma
(right
conduct
appropriate
to
one's
role
and
stage
of
life).
The
caste
system
(varna)
is
embedded
in
classical
Hindu
texts,
but
it
has
been
challenged
from
within,
by
the
Bhakti
movements,
by
Ambedkar's
Dalit
Buddhism,
and
by
the
Indian
constitution.

Jainism:
ahimsa
and
the
most

With
around
4–6
million
adherents
(almost
all
in
India),
Jainism
is
tiny
by
count
but
immense
in
influence:
Gandhi's
doctrine
of
non-violence
drew
directly
on
Jain
thought.
The
core
commitment
is
ahimsa:
non-harm
to
any
living
being,
including
insects,
which
is
why
monks
of
the
Digambara
sect
sweep
the
ground
before
walking.
Karma
in
Jainism
is
not
metaphorical
but
literal:
physical
particles
that
stick
to
the
soul
through
bad
actions,
weighing
it
down.
Liberation
(moksha)
comes
through
shedding
all
karma,
which
requires
extreme
asceticism.
The
Digambara
("sky-clad")
and
Shvetambara
("white-clad")
sects
differ
on
whether
monks
must
be
naked
to
truly
renounce
the
world.
Jainism
has
no
creator
god;
the
universe
is
eternal
and
self-sustaining.
Sikhism:

Founded
by
Guru

(1469–1539)
in

Punjab,
Sikhism
emerged

and

one

God
(expressed
in
"Ik

One
God),
the

of
all
humans,
and
service
(seva)
as

core
spiritual
practice.
The
langar,
a
free
community
kitchen
attached
to
every
Sikh
temple,
feeds
millions
daily
with
no
religious
qualification
required;
it
is
among
the
world's
largest
voluntary
feeding
operations.
The
Khalsa
(the
community
of
initiated
Sikhs,
founded
1699)
is
marked
by
the
Five
Ks:
kesh
(uncut
hair),
kanga
(wooden
comb),
kara
(steel
bracelet),
kachera
(cotton
undergarment),
kirpan
(ceremonial
steel
sword).
There
are
roughly
25–30
million
Sikhs
worldwide,
two-thirds
in
the
Punjab
region
of
India.

Indigenous

Roughly
300
million
people
worldwide
practice
Indigenous
spiritual
traditions,
though
the

is
so

that

is

Animism
(the
belief
that
animals,
plants,
rivers,
mountains,
and
other
entities
have
spiritual
agency)
is
probably
humanity's
oldest
religious
form;
archaeological
evidence
(grave
goods,
cave
paintings
with
animal-human
hybrids)
suggests
it
goes
back
at
least
100,000
years.
Shamanism
is
the
specialist
role
of
mediating
between
the
human
community
and
the
spirit
world,
found
across
Siberia,
the
Americas,
Central
Asia,
and
many
other
regions
with
no
historical
connection.
Common
features:
oral
transmission
(no
scripture),
sacred
geography
(specific
places
are
spiritually
charged),
reciprocity
with
non-human
persons,
and
seasonal
ritual
cycles.
These
traditions
are
not
primitive
precursors
to
"real"
religion:
many
are
philosophically
sophisticated
responses
to
the
question
of
how
humans
should
relate
to
the
living
world
they
depend
on.
Ancient
polytheisms:
approximate
periods
of
cultural
dominance
Greek
and
Roman
religion
was
not
primarily
about
belief:
no
creed
was
required.
Participation
in
civic
ritual
was
compulsory,
but
private
theology
was
free.
The
mystery
religions
(Eleusinian
mysteries,
Mithraism,
Orphism)
offered
personal
transformation
experiences
closer
to
what
modern
people
expect
from
religion.
They
declined
for
complex
reasons:
imperial
Christianity's
coercive
power
from
380
CE
(Theodosius
I
made
Nicene
Christianity
the
Roman
state
religion),
the
appeal
of
universal
monotheism
over
local
pantheons,
and
intellectual
critiques
from
philosophers
who
had
long
been
sceptical
of
the
Olympians.

Atheism,

Atheism
(no
god)
and
agnosticism
(unknowable
whether
there
is
a
god)
are
often
conflated
but
conceptually
distinct:
agnosticism
is
an
epistemic
claim
about
knowledge,

is
a
metaphysical
claim
about
existence.
You
can
be
an
agnostic
atheist
(no
belief
in
god,
and
the
question
is
not
answerable)
or
an
agnostic
theist
(belief
in
god,
but
acknowledged
as
unprovable).
"New
Atheism"
(associated
with
Dawkins,
Hitchens,
Harris,
and
Dennett
from
roughly
2004)
argued
religion
is
not
just
mistaken
but
actively
harmful
and
deserves
no
special
social
deference.
Secular
humanism
is
the
positive
programme:
basing
ethics
on
human
flourishing
and
reason
without
any
supernatural
grounding.
About
1.2
billion
people
self-identify
as
non-religious
(2024),
but
surveys
consistently
find
that
a
large
portion
of
"nones"
still
hold
supernatural
beliefs
(in
fate,
spiritual
forces,
or
life
after
death)
making
them
"believing
without
belonging"
rather
than
committed
materialists.

Secularisation:
is
religion
declining?
The
secularisation
thesis,
formulated
by

like
Bryan
Wilson
and
Peter
Berger
in
the
1960s,
predicted
that
as
societies
modernise
and
science
explains
more,
religion
would
wither.
The
data
since
then
has
forced
major
revisions.
Western

Historically,

Scholars
