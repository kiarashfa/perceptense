Benefits:

The
universal
rule
for
resistance
training
Inhale
on
the
eccentric
phase
The
eccentric
is
the
lowering/lengthening/easier
phase:
where
you're
resisting
gravity.
Breathe
in
here.
Examples:
lowering
the
bar
to
chest
in
bench
press;
descending
into
a
squat;
lowering
yourself
on
a
pull-up;
descending
in
a
deadlift.
Exhale
on
the
concentric
phase
The
concentric
is
the
lifting/shortening/harder
phase:
where
you're
doing
the
work
against
gravity.
Breathe
out
here.
Examples:
pressing
the
bar
up
in
bench;
standing
up
from
a
squat;
pulling
yourself
up
on
a
pull-up;
lifting
the
bar
in
a
deadlift.
Why
this
matters:
intra-abdominal
pressure
(IAP):
Exhaling
during
the
hardest
part
of
a
lift
(concentric)
reduces
intra-abdominal
pressure,
which
reduces
spinal
stability,
making
the
lift
weaker
and
increasing
injury
risk.
The
technical
technique
for
heavy
lifting
is
the
Valsalva
manoeuvre:
take
a
deep
breath,
brace
your
core
hard
(like
you're
about
to
be
punched),
hold
the
breath
through
the
sticking
point
of
the
lift
(the
hardest
moment),
then
exhale
as
you
complete
the
rep.
This
creates
maximum
IAP
and
spinal
stability.
Squat
breathing
cue
Inhale
and
brace
before

Hold
breath
through
the

and
begin
exhale

drive

Complete

at

Reset

each

for

Deadlift
breathing
cue
Stand

brace

Initiate

Begin

as
bar
passes

Exhale
fully

Lower

bar,
reset.

For

Don't

For

(inhale

The

The

Mechanical

(load

When

(the

in
the

detect

(mTOR

The

Exercise

(MPS):
the

to

and

to

MPS
is

for
24–48h
after

Without

the

can't

the
new
tissue
regardless

training
stimulus.
The
limit:
genetics
and
testosterone
Natural

gain:
~0.5–1
kg/month
for
beginners,
~0.25–0.5
kg/month
intermediate,
~0.1–0.25
kg/month

Men
gain
faster
than
women
(testosterone).
Genetic
ceiling
exists:
"natural"

hit

set

and

Protein

The
"anabolic

(eat

is

Total

matters

than

That

distributing

over
3–4

maximises
MPS:
each
meal
should

~20–40g

to

MPS.
Part
F
·
recovery:
the
most
undervalued
variable
Why
muscles
grow
on
rest
days,
not
training
days
Training
is
the
stimulus,
not
the
adaptation
Exercise
creates
microscopic
damage
in
muscle
fibres
(microtears,
especially
from
eccentric
loading).
The
repair
process,
which
happens
primarily
during
sleep
and
rest,
makes
the
fibre
thicker
and
stronger
than
before.
Training
without
recovery
produces
more
damage
than
repair.
"Overtraining"
is
genuinely
possible:
it
suppresses
immune
function,
raises
cortisol
chronically,
and
can
reverse
progress.
Minimum
recovery
time
by
muscle
group
48–72
hours
between
sessions
for
the
same
muscle
Large
muscles
(legs,
back):
72h
minimum
between
heavy
sessions.
Smaller
muscles
(arms,
shoulders):
48h.
High
frequency
(hitting
a
muscle
3x/week)
works
only
if
volume
per
session
is
lower.
Signs
of
insufficient
recovery:
persistent
soreness,
performance
declining
session
to
session,
disrupted
sleep,
mood
changes,
loss
of
motivation.
Sleep's
role
in
muscle
growth
~70–80%
of
growth
hormone
secreted
in
N3
sleep
N3
(deep)
sleep
triggers
the
largest
GH
pulse
of
the
day.
GH
drives
tissue
repair
and
protein
synthesis.
Consistently
sleeping
less
than
7
hours:
impaired
recovery,
lower
testosterone
(~10–15%
reduction
after
one
week
of
5-hour
nights),
elevated
cortisol,
and
reduced
muscle
protein
synthesis.
Sleep
is
arguably
as
important
as
training
for
body
composition.
Progressive
overload:
the
only
principle
that
matters
long-term
Gradually
increasing
the
demand
on
the
muscle
The
body
adapts
to
any
given
load
and
stops
responding.
Progress
requires
progressive
overload:
increasing
weight,
reps,
sets,
range
of
motion,
or
reducing
rest
time
over
time.
Without
it,
training
maintains
but
doesn't
improve.
The
simplest
implementation:
add
a
small
amount
of
weight
(2.5–5
kg)
when
you
can
complete
all
reps
of
all
sets
with
good
form.
DOMS:
delayed
onset
muscle
soreness
Not
an
indicator
of
a
good
workout
DOMS
peaks
24–72h
after
exercise,
especially
after
eccentric-heavy
movements
or
novel
exercises.
It
is
inflammation
from
microscopic
damage,
not
a
measure
of
training
quality.
You
can
have
an
excellent,
muscle-building
session
with
little
DOMS
(particularly
as
you
become
more
trained).
Chasing
soreness
is
not
a
training
strategy.
DOMS
is
not
related
to
lactic
acid,
that
clears
within
~30
min
of
exercise
ending.
Overtraining
vs
underrecovery
Most
"overtraining"
is
actually
under-eating
and
under-sleeping
True
overtraining
syndrome
(systemic
physiological

from
excessive

volume)
is

athletes.
More

functional
overreaching:
a

of
fatigue
where
performance
drops,
usually

training
hard
while
eating
in
a
deficit
and
sleeping
poorly.
Fix:
eat
more,
sleep
more,
reduce
training
volume
temporarily.
Signs
of
real
concern:
persistent
performance
decline,
resting
heart
rate
chronically
elevated,
mood
disturbance,
illness
frequency.

Part
J
·
common
myths
&
what
the
evidence
actually
says
❌
"Lifting
makes
women
bulky"
Women
have
~10–20×
less
testosterone
than
men
and
significantly
less
growth
hormone.
The
hormonal
environment
for
dramatic
muscle
growth
simply
doesn't
exist.
Female
competitive
bodybuilders
achieve
their
physique
after
years
of
specific,
high-volume
training
often
combined
with
performance-enhancing
drugs.
Resistance
training
for
women
produces
strength,
bone
density,
improved
body
composition,
and
metabolic
benefit,
not
bulk.
❌
"You
can
spot-reduce
fat"
Doing
500
sit-ups
will
not
burn
the
fat
on
your
stomach.
Fat
loss
is
systemic:
the
body
decides
where
to
draw
down
fat
stores
based
on
genetics
and
hormones,
not
which
muscles
you
work.
The
stomach,
hips,
and
thighs
tend
to
store
fat
preferentially
(especially
in
women)
and
are
the
last
places
to
lean
out.
Core
exercises
build
core
muscles;
they
do
not
"burn"
overlying
fat.
Only
a
calorie
deficit
reduces
fat,
and
where
it
comes
off
first
is
not
controllable.
❌
"Muscle
turns
into
fat
when
you
stop
training"
Muscle
and
fat
are
different
tissues:
one
cannot
convert
into
the
other.
When
you
stop
training:
muscle
undergoes
atrophy
(the
fibres
shrink)
due
to
lack
of
mechanical
stimulus.
Simultaneously,
if
you
continue
eating
the
same
amount
without
the
energy
expenditure
of
training,
you
gain
fat.
These
two
things
happen
at
the
same
time,
giving
the
appearance
of
one
"turning
into"
the
other.
They
are
independent
processes.
❌
"Cardio
kills
your
gains"
The
"interference
effect"
(cardio
blunting
muscle
growth)
exists
but
is
heavily
overstated
by
the
fitness
internet.
Meta-analyses
show
that
concurrent
training
(cardio
+
weights
in
the
same
program)
reduces
hypertrophy
by
only
~5–10%
compared
to
resistance
training
alone,
and
only
when
cardio
volume
is
excessive
(long
sessions,
same
day
as
lifting,
same
muscle
groups).
3–4
moderate
cardio
sessions
per
week
alongside
resistance
training
does
not
meaningfully
impair
muscle
growth
and
improves
health
markers
significantly.
❌
"You
need
to
feel
the
burn
/
soreness
to
have
trained
well"
The
burn
(metabolic
acidosis
from
lactate
accumulation)
and
soreness
(DOMS
from
eccentric
damage)
are
both
side
effects
of
training,
not
measures
of
effectiveness.
An
experienced
lifter
can
have
a
highly
productive
session
with
minimal
soreness.
Chasing
the
burn
leads
to
prioritising
metabolic
stress
over
mechanical
tension,
which
is
a
less
effective
stimulus
for
strength
and
hypertrophy.
Quality
of
movement
and
progressive
overload
matter
far
more
than
how
much
something
hurts.
❌
"Static
stretching
before
exercise
prevents
injury"
Static
stretching
(hold
a
stretch
for
30–60
seconds)
before
exercise
has
been
shown
to
temporarily
reduce
muscle
force
output,
reduce
peak
power,
and
reduce
sprint
performance:
the
opposite
of
what
you
want
before
lifting
or
sprinting.
The
evidence
for
injury
prevention
from
pre-exercise
static
stretching
is
weak.
The
effective
warm-up
approach:
dynamic
movements
(arm
circles,
leg
swings,
bodyweight
squats,
light
sets
of
the
exercise
you're
about
to
perform).
Static
stretching
is
valuable:
just
save
it
for
after
training,
or
as
a
standalone
flexibility
session.

2.
What
is
the
difference
between
a
squat
and
a
deadlift:
why
do
both
exist,
and
which
muscles
does
each
primarily
work?
Show
answer
They
target
overlapping
but
distinct
movement
patterns.
The
squat
is
primarily
a
knee-dominant
pattern:
the
knee
flexes
and
extends
under
load,
with
the
torso
relatively
upright.
Primary
movers:
quadriceps
(front
of
thigh),
glutes,
with
hamstrings
and
adductors
supporting.
The
bar
(or
weight)
is
above
the
centre
of
mass,
requiring
upper
back
and
core
to
stabilise.
The
deadlift
is
primarily
a
hip-dominant
pattern:
the
hip
hinges
(bends),
with
the
spine
in
a
neutral
position
and
the
bar
on
the
floor.
Primary
movers:
hamstrings,
glutes,
erector
spinae
(lower
back),
with
quadriceps
helping
to
break
the
floor
initially.
Secondary:
traps,
lats
(to
keep
bar
close),
forearms
(grip).
Why
both
exist:
the
squat
develops
anterior
chain
(quads)
and
vertical
pushing
power;
the
deadlift
develops
posterior
chain
(hamstrings,
glutes,
back)
and
pulling
strength.
Together

cover
essentially
all
major
lower
body
and
hip

A
programme
with
both
is

than

3.
What
is
EPOC
and
why
does
it
make

complicated?
Show
answer
EPOC
(Excess
Post-exercise
Oxygen
Consumption)
is
the
elevated
oxygen
consumption,
and
therefore
elevated
calorie
burn,
that
persists
after
exercise
ends.
After
aerobic
exercise,
EPOC
lasts
15–60
minutes
and
adds
a
modest
number
of
calories
(50–150
kcal).
After
high-intensity
resistance
training
or
HIIT,
EPOC
can
last
24–48
hours
and
add
100–350
kcal.
The
complication
for
calorie
tracking:
fitness
trackers
and
apps
calculate
calories
burned
during
exercise
based
on
the
movement,
but
they
don't
account
for
EPOC.
More
importantly,
heavy
resistance
exercise
has
a
smaller
"during
exercise"
calorie
burn
than
cardio
(because
rest
periods
are
included)
but
significantly
larger
post-exercise
metabolic
elevation.
This
is
why
pure
calorie-during-exercise
comparisons
favour
cardio
but
miss
the
long-term
metabolic
advantage
of
resistance
training.
The
practical
implication:
two
workouts
with
the
same
calorie
burn
during
exercise
may
have
very
different
total
metabolic
costs
once
EPOC
is
included.
4.
Why

Show

Several

First,
glycogen

and

to

Each

Increased

within

Second,

that

(the

and

This

Third,

The

For

5.

6

same

same

What

Show

A

after
6

often
in

First

most
common:
absence
of

overload.
If
you've
been

the
same
weight
for
the
same

for
months,
the
muscle
has
fully
adapted
to
that

and
has
no
reason
to
grow

Fix:
systematically
add
small

of
weight
(2.5

or

or
add

Second:
insufficient

Muscle

If

not

can

Fix:
track

Third:

If

6

Fix:

ensure

A
less

fourth:
your

needs

change
(adding
volume,
changing

to
create
novel

adjusting

True

is
rare;
the
other
three
causes
are

more
common.
6.
What
does
"training
to
failure"
mean,
and
should
you
do
it?
Show
answer
Training
to
failure
means
performing
reps
until
you
physically
cannot
complete
another
rep
with
proper
form
(technical
failure).
"Reps
in
reserve"
(RIR)
is
the
alternative
language:
1
RIR
means
stopping
with
1
rep
left
in
the
tank.
Research
shows
that
going
to
failure
and
stopping
1–3
reps
short
of
failure
produce
very
similar
hypertrophy:
the
last
few
reps
of
a
hard
set
are
the
most
stimulating,
but
absolute
failure
isn't
required
to
get
that
stimulus.
Practical
considerations
against
always
training
to
failure:
(1)
it
significantly
increases
recovery
time,
(2)
it
increases
injury
risk
(form
breaks
down
at
failure),
(3)
it
makes
it
harder
to
maintain
technique
across
multiple
sets,
(4)
CNS
fatigue
accumulates.
The
modern
evidence-based
approach:
most
working
sets
should
end
1–2
reps
short
of
failure,
with
occasional
sets
taken
to
failure
on
low-risk
exercises
(machines,
cables,
isolation
movements).
Never
train
to
failure
on
technical
free-weight
movements
like
squats
and
deadlifts.
7.
Is
there
any
meaningful
difference
between
free
weights
and
machines
for
building
muscle?
Show
answer
For
pure
hypertrophy
(muscle
size),
machines
and
free
weights
produce
similar
results
when
sets
are
taken
close
to
failure
at
matched
volumes.
The
practical
differences
are
significant
however.
Free
weights
(barbells,
dumbbells)
require
stabiliser
muscles
to
control
the
movement,
train
neuromuscular
coordination,
and
are
more
transferable
to
athletic
performance
and
real-world
strength.
They
also
have
a
steeper
learning
curve
and
higher
injury
risk
when
technique
is
poor.
Machines
provide
guided
movement
(less
stabiliser
demand),
allow
isolation
of
specific
muscles,
are
safer
for
training
close
to
failure
(no
fear
of
dropping
a
bar),
and
are
easier
for
beginners
to
learn.
The
practical
optimal
approach:
compound
free
weight
movements
(squat,
deadlift,
bench,
row)
as
the
foundation,
with
machines
and
cables
as
accessories
for
isolation
and
volume.
The
internet
debate
about
"free
weights
vs
machines"
is
largely
irrelevant:
what
matters
is
progressive
overload
applied
consistently
over
time.
