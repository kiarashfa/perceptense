The

Music
is
sound
organised
in
time.
That
deceptively
simple
definition
conceals
its
full
strangeness:
music
works
through
physics
(vibrations
at
specific
frequencies),
mathematics
(rhythmic
relationships,
harmonic
ratios),
culture
(learned
systems
of
expectation),
and
biology
(emotional
responses
hardwired
into
the
auditory
system).
A
symphony
is
acoustic
waves
in
air,
integer
ratios
of
frequency,
centuries
of
convention,
and
—
if
it
works
—
something
that
makes
you
weep,
or
march,
or
feel
less
alone.
Music
is
universal
in
that
every
known
human
culture
makes
it;
it
is
not
universal
in
that
what
counts
as
music,
what
sounds
"right,"
and
what
emotions
are
evoked
differ
radically
across
cultures
and
historical
periods.

What
it
is
Pitch
is
the
perceived
frequency
of
a
sound.
A
string
vibrating
440
times
per
second
(440
Hz)
produces
A4
—
the
tuning
standard.
Double
the
frequency
(880
Hz)
and
you
get
A5:
an
octave
higher.
The
relationship
between
frequencies
is
not
arbitrary:
notes
sharing
simple
integer
ratios
(2:1,
3:2,
4:3)
sound
consonant
to
most
listeners.
This
is
physics,
not
culture
—
though
which
pitches
are
used
and
how
they
relate
is
highly
cultural.
Scale
systems
Western
music
divides
the
octave
into
12
equal
semitones
(equal
temperament
—
a
compromise
allowing
the
same
piece
to
be
played
in
all
keys).
Indian
classical
music
uses
microtones
between
those
12.
Arabic
maqam
uses
quarter-tones.
Pentatonic
scales
(5
notes
per
octave)
appear
in
music
from
China,
Scotland,
West
Africa,
and
the
Andes.
The
12-tone
Western
system
is
one
solution
among
many,
not
the
natural
order.
Harmony
Multiple

The

(stable)
or

(unstable,

forms

of
Western
harmonic
theory.

—

"home"

from

1600

1900.
Atonality
(Schoenberg,
1908)

Pulse

Rhythm
is
the
organisation
of
sound
in
time.
The
pulse
(beat)
is
the
underlying
regular
heartbeat
of
music.
Metre
groups
beats
into
recurring
patterns:
4/4
(four
beats
per
bar
—
the
most
common
in
Western
popular
music),
3/4
(waltz
time),
6/8
(compound
duple),
5/4
(Dave
Brubeck's
Take
Five),
7/8
(Balkan
folk
music).
Syncopation
Emphasis
on
the
weak
beats
rather
than
the
strong
ones.
The
foundation
of
jazz,
blues,
funk,
and
most
African-derived
popular
music.
A
4/4
bar
normally
stresses
beats
1
and
3;
syncopation
stresses
2
and
4
(the
backbeat
of
rock
and
roll),
or
inserts
accents
between
the
main
beats.
Syncopation
creates
the
"groove"
by
placing
emphasis
where
the
listener
doesn't
expect
it.

(BPM).
Marked

(very

(walking

76–108

(fast,

(very

168–200

Human
resting

is
60–80
BPM
—
music
at
this

natural;
faster

slower

What

Timbre
is
the
quality
or
colour
of
a
sound
—
what
distinguishes
a
violin
from
a
flute
playing
the
same
note
at
the
same
volume.
It
is
determined
by
the
harmonic
overtones
present
in
the
sound.
Every
instrument
produces
not
just
the
fundamental
frequency
but
a
series
of
overtones
above
it;
the
relative
strength
of
these
overtones
creates
the
characteristic
sound.
Orchestration
The
art
of
combining
different
timbres.
Which
instruments
play
which
notes,
in
which
register,
with
what
articulation.
Berlioz,
Rimsky-Korsakov,
Debussy,
and
Ravel
were
masters
of
orchestration.
Debussy
described
it
as
"painting
in
sound"
—
the
same
note
played
by
a
horn,
a
flute,
and
a
violin
are
three
different
compositional
choices.
Extended
techniques
20th
century
music
expanded
the
timbre
palette:
prepared
piano
(Cage
—
objects
placed
between
strings),
sul
ponticello
(bowing
near
the
bridge
for
a
glassy
tone),
multiphonics
(producing
two
pitches
simultaneously
on
a
wind
instrument),
electronic
synthesis,
and
digital
sampling.
Electronic
music
expands
the
timbral
universe
to
any
imaginable
sound.
Volume
and
expression
Dynamics
is
the
variation
in
loudness.
Marked
in
Italian:
ppp
(very
very
soft)
through
pp,
p,
mp,
mf,
f,
ff,
fff
(very
very
loud).
Crescendo
=
gradually
louder;
diminuendo
=
gradually
softer.
Dynamic
contrast
is
one
of
music's
most
powerful
expressive
tools
—
the
Mannheim
crescendo
(18th-century
innovation)
was
considered
startling
and
thrilling
when
first
heard.
Articulation
How
notes
are
begun
and
ended.
Staccato
(short,
detached),
legato
(smooth,
connected),
marcato
(heavily
accented),
tenuto
(held
for

(sudden

A
melody
played

versus

uses
the
same
pitches
at
the
same
rhythm
—
but
feels
completely
different.
Articulation
is
the
difference
between
a
mechanical
performance
and
a
musical
one.
Silence
Silence
is

Rests

John

Beethoven's

Melody
A
melody
is
a
sequence
of
pitches
perceived
as
a
single
coherent
line
—
the
part
you
hum
afterwards.
What
makes
a
melody
memorable
is
not
randomness
or
complexity
but
shape:
ascent
and
descent,
tension
and
release,
repetition
and
variation.
Mozart's
melodies
feel
inevitable
because
each
note
seems
like
the
only
possible
next
note;
his
gift
was
disguising
the
complexity
of
choice
as
simplicity.
A
melody's
contour
(the
overall
shape
of
rises
and
falls),
its
range
(how
wide
an
interval
it
spans),
and
its
rhythm
are
each
independent
variables
a
composer
controls.
The
opening
four
notes
of
Beethoven's
5th
Symphony
(G-G-G-Eb)
are
a
rhythmic
motif,
not
a
melody
—
the
distinction
matters.
Counterpoint
Two
or
more
independent
melodic
lines
played
simultaneously.
Unlike
harmony
(chords
—
vertical
combinations),
counterpoint
is
horizontal
—
each
line
has
its
own
shape
and
momentum,
yet
they
fit
together
according
to
rules
governing
consonance
and
dissonance.
J.S.
Bach
is
the
supreme
master:
his
fugues
can
have
up
to
five
independent
voices,
each
pursuing
its
own
melodic
argument
while
simultaneously
creating
a
coherent
harmonic
whole.
The
opening
of
the
Well-Tempered
Clavier's
Fugue
in
C
minor
(WTC
Book
I)
shows
a
single
theme
entered
four
times,
staggered,
until
all
voices
are
in
motion
at
once.
Counterpoint
is
logic
made
audible.
Texture
The
density
and
layering
of
musical
lines.
Monophony:
a
single
melodic
line
with
no
accompaniment
(Gregorian
chant).
Homophony:
one
melody
with
chordal
accompaniment
(virtually
all
pop
music).
Polyphony:
multiple
simultaneous
melodic
lines
of
roughly
equal
importance
(Bach
fugues).
Heterophony:
multiple
versions
of
the
same
melody
played
simultaneously
with
slight
variations
(common
in
Gamelan
music).
Western
classical
music
moves
freely
between
textures
for
structural
and
expressive
effect
—
a

to

can

devastating.

Western

begins

Gregorian
chant
(codified
around
590–604
CE
under
Pope
Gregory
I)
established

From

progressively

(organum,
~900

(the

~1200),

(Renaissance,
1400–1600),

(Baroque

1820–1900),

(Modernism,
1900–present).
Each

built
on
and
reacted
against
the
previous
one.
The
complete
story
is
not
one
of
simple
"progress"
but
of
oscillation
between
complexity
and
clarity,
rule
and
freedom.

how

listen
—
building
an
active
ear
Listening
is
a
skill
Most
people
hear
music
passively
—
as
emotional
atmosphere
or
background.
Active
listening
means
attending
to
specific
elements:
following
a
melodic
line
through
an
ensemble,
noticing
when
harmony
changes
and
what
effect
the
change
has,
identifying
the
structural
moment
(is
this
the
exposition
or
the
development?),
hearing
two
independent
contrapuntal
voices
simultaneously.
This
is
not
an
innate
talent
—
it
is
trained.
Concert-goers
in
the
18th
century
routinely
talked,
ate,
and
moved
around
during
performances;
the
convention
of
respectful
silence
is
itself
a
historical
construction,
established
in
the
19th
century
with
the
rise
of
Romantic
concert
culture.
Active
listening
transforms
the
experience
of
music
from
pleasant
sensation

What

It

Schoenberg's

Swing

show

Because

--

--

Even
within

different

and

Among

of

than

tightly

of
a
French

mellows
and
disperses
the

before
they
leave

Construction

body

and
even

player's

filter

ways.
This
is
why

same

produce

change

show
answer
The

invented
around

Cristofori,
replaced

as

by
the

Its

the

which
sounds

same

of
how

the

responds
to

--
"piano"
and
"forte"
(soft
and

are
literally
built
into
its
name.
Beethoven
exploited
the
piano's
dynamic
range
and
sustaining
power
in
ways
impossible
on
the
harpsichord.
Chopin
discovered
that
the
piano
could
sing
--
long,
arching
melodic
lines
supported
by
rich
harmonic
masses
held
down
by
the
sustain
pedal.
The
piano
also
democratised
music
theory:
having
all
88
notes
laid
out
in
a
single
visual
plane
made
it
the
universal
tool
for
teaching
harmony,
composition,
and
music
theory.
Virtually
every
major
Western
composer
from
1780
onward
either
composed
at
the
piano
or
composed
with
its
logic
in
mind.
14.
Does

have
meaning,
or
do
we
project
meaning
onto
it?
show
answer
Both,
in
different
senses.
The
debate
between
"absolutist"
and
"referentialist"
positions
has
run
for
200
years.
Absolutists
(Hanslick,
Stravinsky)
argue
that
music
is
purely
about
itself
—
patterns
of
sound
in
time
—
and
any
external
meaning
is
imposed
by
the
listener.
Referentialists
argue
that
music
inherently
expresses
something
beyond
itself.
The
evidence
suggests
a
middle
path:
some
musical
features
—
fast
tempo,
major
mode,
high
register,
conjunct
melodic
motion
—
are
reliably
associated
with
positive
emotion
across
cultures,
suggesting
a
physical
basis.
But
the
specific
narrative
content
("this
is
a
storm,"
"this
is
love")
is
always
partially
projection.
The
philosophical
question
is
unresolved;
the
practical
question
is
simpler:
both
modes
of
listening
(structural
attention
and
emotional
response)
are
valid,
and
the
richest
listening

Some

The

18.

show

Acoustic

The

Concert

room

Electronic

--

with

cannot

The
two
traditions

they

different
problems.

What

show
answer
Stradivari

(1666-1737)

The

(the

Stradivari
used
grew
in

unusual
cold

wood

(the

This

fully

only
