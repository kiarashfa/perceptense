When

Part
J
·
logical
fallacies
everyone
should
know
A
fallacy
is
an
argument
that
appears
to
support
a
conclusion
but
doesn't
actually
succeed.
Recognising
them
is
one
of
the
most
practically
useful
skills
philosophy
gives
you.
Ad
hominem
against
the
person
Attacking
the
arguer
rather
than
the
argument.
Even
if
the
speaker
is
dishonest
or
hypocritical,
their
argument
must
be
addressed
on
its
own
terms.
Example:
"You
can't
trust
his
climate
science:
he
drives
an
SUV."
Straw
man
homo
stramineus
Misrepresenting
someone's
position
to
make
it
easier
to
attack,
then
defeating
the
misrepresentation
rather
than
the
actual
view.
Example:
"Environmentalists
want
us
to
go
back
to
living
in
caves."
False
dilemma
bifurcatio
Presenting
only
two
options
as
if
they're
exhaustive,
when
other
possibilities
exist.
Example:
"You're
either
with
us
or
against
us."
Appeal
to
authority
argumentum
ad
verecundiam
Claiming
something
is
true
because
an
authority
figure
said
so,
without
evaluating
the
evidence.
Legitimate
when
the
authority
is
genuinely
expert
in
the
relevant
domain.
Example:
"A
Nobel
laureate
says
homeopathy
works."
Slippery
slope
camel's
nose
Claiming
that
one
event
will
inevitably
lead
to
extreme
consequences
without
showing
why
each
step
in
the
chain
is
probable.
Example:
"Legalising
cannabis
will
lead
to
everyone
taking
heroin."
Post
hoc
ergo
propter
hoc
after
this,
therefore
because
of
this
Inferring
causation
from
correlation:
A
happened,
then
B
happened,
therefore
A
caused
B.
Example:
"I
wore
my
lucky
socks
and
we
won
the
match."
Begging
the
question
petitio
principii
Assuming
the
conclusion
in
one
of
the
premises:
circular
reasoning.
The
argument
is
valid
only
if
you
already
accept
what
you're
trying
to
prove.
Example:
"The
Bible
is
true
because
it
says
so
in
the
Bible."
Appeal
to
nature
argumentum
ad
naturam
Assuming
that
what
is
natural
is
good
and
what
is
artificial
is
bad.
Nature
contains
arsenic,
viruses,
and
parasites;
artificial
penicillin
saves
lives.
Example:
"This
supplement
is
safe:
it's
all
natural."
Is-ought
fallacy
Hume's
guillotine
Deriving
a
normative
"ought"
from
a
descriptive
"is"
without
justification.
Facts
alone
cannot
determine
values
(see
Hume,
Part
G).
Example:
"Humans
evolved
to
eat
meat,
so
we
ought
to
eat
meat."
Modus
tollens
(valid)
denying
the
consequent
Not
a
fallacy:
a
valid
form.
If
P→Q
and
¬Q,
then
¬P.
Crucial
for
falsifying
scientific
hypotheses.
Karl
Popper
built
his
philosophy
of
science
on
this
form.
Example:
"If
the
drug
works,
patients
improve.
Patients
didn't
improve.
The
drug
doesn't
work."
Affirming
the
consequent
fallacy
of
the
converse
If
P→Q
and
Q,
one
cannot
conclude
P.
This
is
a
logical
fallacy
that
looks
deceptively
valid,
and
is
the
basis
of
most
conspiracy
reasoning.
Example:
"If
it
rains,
the
ground
is
wet.
The
ground
is
wet.
Therefore
it
rained."
Hasty
generalisation
secundum
quid
Drawing
a
broad
conclusion
from
an
insufficient
sample.
The
larger
the
generalisation,
the
more
evidence
is
required.
Example:
"I
met
two
rude
Parisians.
Parisians
are
rude."
Part
K
·
essential

A
priori
vs

posteriori
Before
vs
after
experience
A
priori
knowledge
is
knowable
through
reason
alone:
"All
bachelors
are
unmarried,"
"2+2=4."
A
posteriori
requires
experience:
"Water
boils
at
100°C."
Kant
argued
the
most
interesting
philosophical
questions
concern
whether
there
is
synthetic
a
priori
knowledge:
claims
that
are
both
informative
and
knowable
without
experience.
Analytic
vs
synthetic
True
by
definition
vs
by
fact
Analytic
propositions
are
true
by
the
meaning
of
their
terms:
"All
triangles
have
three
sides."
Synthetic
propositions
add
new
information:
"The
cat
is
on
the
mat."
Hume's
fork:
all
meaningful
claims
are
either
analytic
or
synthetic.
Claims
that
are
neither
are
meaningless,
which
eliminated
much
traditional
metaphysics.
Ontology
The
study
of
what
exists
A
branch
of
metaphysics
concerned
with
the
fundamental
categories
of
existence.
Do
numbers
exist?
Do
properties
exist?
Do
abstract
objects
(justice,
redness)
exist?
Ontological
commitments
are
the
entities
your
theory
says
must
exist.
"To
be
is
to
be
the
value
of
a
variable":
Quine's
criterion.
Empiricism
vs
rationalism
The
great
epistemological
split
Rationalists
(Descartes,
Leibniz,
Spinoza):
reason
is
the
primary
source
of
knowledge;
some
knowledge
is
innate.
Empiricists
(Locke,
Hume,
Berkeley):
all
knowledge
derives
from
sensory
experience.
Kant's
critical
philosophy
synthesised
both:
experience
provides
content,
reason
provides
structure.
Dialectic
Reasoning
through
contradiction
The
Socratic
method:
reaching
truth
through
question
and
answer,
exposing
contradictions.
Hegel's
dialectic:
thesis
→
antithesis
→
synthesis.
Marx
inverted
Hegel's
idealist
dialectic
into
a
materialist
one:
history
progresses
through
class
conflict,
not
the
clash
of
ideas.
Phenomenology
The
study
of
conscious
experience
Founded
by
Husserl;
developed
by
Heidegger
and
Merleau-Ponty.
Describes
the
structure
of
experience
from
the
first-person
perspective.
Heidegger:
we
don't
first
perceive
a
hammer
then
decide
to
use
it:
we
encounter
it
as
already
"ready-to-hand,"
embedded
in
a
context
of
purposes.
Qualia
The
"what
it
is
like"
of
experience
The
subjective,
intrinsic
properties
of
conscious
experience:
the
redness
of
red,
the
painfulness
of
pain,
the
taste
of
coffee.
Even
a
complete
physical
description
of
a
brain
state
seems
to
leave
something
out:
the
felt
quality.
This
explanatory
gap
is
the
"hard
problem
of
consciousness"
(Chalmers,
1995).
Teleology
Purpose-directed
explanation
Explaining
things
in
terms
of
their
ends
or
purposes.
Aristotle's
final
cause.
Biology
and
ethics
are
heavily
teleological.
The
modern
scientific
revolution
largely
replaced
teleological
with
mechanistic
explanation
in
physics
and
chemistry,
but
teleology
resists
elimination
in
biology
(what
is
the
heart
for?)
and
ethics
(what
is
the
good
life
for?).
Reductionism
vs

Parts
vs

Reductionism:
complex
phenomena
can
be
fully
explained
by
their

components
(consciousness
=
brain
states
=

=

Holism:
wholes

properties
that
cannot
be
derived
from
their
parts
alone.
The
mind-body
problem
turns
partly
on
whether

states
are

to
physical
states.

Part
L
·
ethics
and
moral
reasoning:
going
deeper
Metaethics:
what
are
moral
claims
actually
doing?
Before
we
ask
what
is
right
,
metaethics
asks:
what
kind
of
thing
is
a
moral
claim?
Are
moral
statements
true
or
false?
Are
moral
properties
real
features
of
the
world,
or
something
we
project
onto
it?
Moral
realism
Moral
facts
exist
independently
of
what
anyone
thinks
or
feels.
"Torturing
innocents
for
fun
is
wrong"
is
as
objectively
true
as
"2+2=4."
Key
figures:
Plato
(the
Form
of
the
Good),
G.E.
Moore
(moral
properties
are
non-natural
but
real),
Derek
Parfit
(reasons
are
part
of
the
fabric
of
the
universe).
The
challenge:
how
do
we
perceive
moral
facts?
What
kind
of
thing
is
a
moral
property?
Error
theory
Moral
claims
purport
to
be
objective
facts
but
they
are
all
false,
because
there
are
no
moral
facts
(J.L.
Mackie,
1977).
Moral
language
has
a
built-in
pretension
to
objectivity
that
reality
cannot
satisfy.
This
is
a
form
of
moral
anti-realism.
It
is
radical
but
internally
consistent:
we
can
explain
why
humans
make
moral
claims
without
any
moral
facts
existing.
Expressivism
(non-cognitivism)
Moral
statements
do
not
assert
facts
at
all;
they
express
attitudes.
"Torture
is
wrong"
means
something
like
"Boo
to
torture!"
(Ayer's
emotivism)
or
expresses
a
commitment
to
norms
that
prohibit
torture
(Blackburn's
quasi-realism,
Gibbard's
norm-expressivism).
The
challenge:
"If
torture
is
wrong,
then
it
is
wrong
to
make
people
do
it"
--
moral
logic
seems
to
require
that
moral
sentences
have
truth
values.
Constructivism
Moral
facts
are
neither
discovered
(realism)
nor
merely
projected
(expressivism)
--
they
are
constructed
by
rational
procedures.
Rawls:
principles
of
justice
are
what
rational
people
would
choose
behind
the
veil
of
ignorance.
Korsgaard:
moral
obligations
arise
from
the
reflective
endorsement
of
one's
own
practical
identity.
The
question:
what
makes
the
constructive
procedure
authoritative?

Applied

--

Moral

Immediate,

Philosophers

Rationalists
(Kant):

Intuitionists
(Ross,

Reflective

Moral

(Thomas

1976)
We

A

Nagel

The

Originating

Aquinas,
developed
by
modern
just

theorists:
it
is
sometimes
permissible
to
cause
a
harm

good

even

be
impermissible

cause
that

intentionally.
Four
conditions:
(1)
the
action
itself
is
not
intrinsically
wrong;
(2)
the
agent
intends
the
good
effect;
(3)
the
bad
effect
is
foreseen
but
not
intended;
(4)
there
is
proportionate
reason.
Used
in:
medical
ethics
(giving
pain-relieving

that
may

death),
military
ethics
(civilian

from

The
separateness
of
persons
Rawls's
objection
to

maximising
aggregate

treats
society
as
if
it
were
a
single
person
and
can
justify
sacrificing
some
individuals
for
others.
But
persons
are
distinct
--
my
suffering
cannot
be
compensated
by
your
happiness.
Rights
and
justice
constrain
the
maximisation
of
aggregate
welfare

they
respect
the
fact
that
each

not

with
others'.
This
is
one

the
most
important

20th-century

Peter

Distance

If

should
--

to

EA

use

to

may

demanding

may

self-undermining;

If

Show

In

"God

Hume's

The

This

The

Bad
faith
(mauvaise

The

(existential

The

("The

"standing

"right

Investigations
(1953),
Wittgenstein
asks
whether
a
language
that
only
one
person
can
understand
(a
"private
language"
for
inner
experiences)
is
possible.
His
argument:
language
requires
rules,
and
rules
require
the
possibility
of
following
them
correctly
or
incorrectly.
But
if
only
I
can
access
my
private
sensations,
no
one
(including
me)
can
verify
whether
I'm
applying
my
private
terms
correctly.
There
is
no
standard
for
correctness,
and
therefore
no
genuine
rule,
and
therefore
no
genuine
language.
"A
wheel
that
can
be
turned
though
nothing
else
moves
with
it,
is
not
part
of
the
mechanism."
This
is
an
attack
on
Cartesian
inner
theatre:
the
idea
that
the
mind
is
a
private
realm
containing
definite
inner
objects
(pains,
images,
thoughts)
that
language
labels.
Language
for
mental
states
gets
its
meaning
from
the
public
circumstances
of
its
use,
not
from
inner
ostensive
definition.
The
argument
matters
because
it
challenges
the
picture
of
mind
that
underlies
much
of
psychology,
AI,
and
philosophy
of
mind:
the
idea
that
there
is
a
determinate
mental
content
that
behaviour
merely
expresses.
If
Wittgenstein
is
right,
the
very
grammar
of
"inner
states"
needs
philosophical
therapy.
9.
What
is
the
difference
between
moral
realism
and
error
theory,
and
why
does
it
matter
for
everyday
moral
argument?
Show
answer
Moral
realism
holds
that
there
are
mind-independent
moral
facts:
"torturing
innocents
for
fun
is
wrong"
is
true
in
the
same
objective
sense
as
"the
Earth
orbits
the
sun."
Different
versions
disagree
on
what
grounds
these
facts
(natural
properties,
non-natural
properties,
divine
commands,
coherent
rational
principles).
Error
theory
(J.L.
Mackie,
Ethics:
Inventing
Right
and
Wrong,
1977)
agrees
with
the
realist
that
moral
claims
purport
to
be
objective
but
argues
they
are
systematically
false,
because
no
moral
facts
exist.
Mackie's
"argument
from
queerness":
if
moral
properties
existed,
they
would
be
metaphysically
strange
entities
--
unlike
anything
else
in
the
natural
world
--
and
we
would
need
a
mysterious
faculty
to
detect
them.
His
"argument
from
relativity":
the
wide
cross-cultural
variation
in
moral
beliefs
is
better
explained
by
different
social
environments
than
by
different
access
to
moral
facts.
Why
it
matters:
if
moral
realism
is
false,
claims
like
"the
Holocaust
was
objectively
wrong"
become
complicated.
Most
non-realists
argue
this
does
not
collapse
into
"anything
goes"
--
expressivists
(Blackburn,
Gibbard)
argue
we
can
be
fully
committed
to
moral
claims,
engage
in
moral
reasoning,
and
criticise
others,
all
without
requiring
moral
facts
in
the
realist
sense.
The
debate
matters
because
it
affects
how
confidently
we
can
appeal
to
moral
considerations
in
political
argument,
law,
and
international
relations.
10.
What
is
the
non-identity
problem,
and
why
does
it
make
environmental
and
intergenerational
ethics
so
philosophically
difficult?
Show
answer
Derek
Parfit
introduced
the
non-identity
problem
in
Reasons
and
Persons
(1984).
Suppose
a
government
adopts
a
policy
of
rapid
resource
depletion
today
that
will
leave
people
in
200
years
worse
off
but
still
with
lives
worth
living.
It
seems
we
have
harmed
those
future
people.
But:
the
very
people
who
will
exist
in
200
years
under
this
policy
are
different
people
from
those
who
would
have
existed
under
a
more
cautious
policy
--
different
energy
policies,
different
social
environments,
different
people
meeting
and
having
different
children.
The
future
people
we
are
comparing
against
would
not
have
existed
at
all
under
the
alternative
policy.
So
how
can
the
policy
harm
them?
You
can
only
harm
a
person
if
you
make
that
specific
person
worse
off
than
they
would
otherwise
have
been.
Since
the
people
born
under
the
"harmful"
policy
could
not
have
existed
without
it,
the
policy
is
not
worse
for
them
--
they
were
never
wronged,
yet
we
feel
the
policy
is
clearly
wrong.
This
creates
a
"repugnant
conclusion"
paradox
(Parfit):
since
we
cannot
harm
future
people
by
causing
them
to
exist
(as
long
as
their
lives
are
worth
living),
it
seems
we
should
create
as
many
people
as
possible
even
at
very
low
welfare
--
a
conclusion
almost
everyone
finds
repugnant
but
is
hard
to
avoid
from
within
standard
consequentialist
frameworks.
The
non-identity
problem
is
one
of
the
deepest
unsolved
problems
in
population
ethics
and
is
philosophically
central
to
climate
change
ethics,
reproductive
ethics,
and
longtermism.
