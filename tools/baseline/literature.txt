Language

The

Literature
is
language
organised
beyond
its
purely
informational
function,
shaped
for
aesthetic
effect,
emotional
resonance,
and
the
creation

It

the
same

as
human
thought.
A

experienced
through

works
through
language,
which
is
already
structured
by

and

This
makes

simultaneously
the
most
intimate
art
form
(it
happens
inside
the
reader's
mind)
and
the
most
culturally
contingent
(it
requires
deep
knowledge
of
a
specific
language).
Homer's
Iliad
has
been
read
for
2,800

not
because
it
provides
information
but
because
it
tells
the
truth
about
war,
glory,
grief,
and
mortality
in
ways
that
transcend
its
original
context.
What
makes
writing
"literary"?
the
key
properties
Density
of
language
Literary
writing
earns
re-reading.
Each
sentence
is
chosen,
not
merely
adequate.
Poetry
maximises
this:
the
poem
is
untranslatable
because
every
word,
sound,
and
position
contributes.
A
news
article
communicates
its
content;
a
poem
is
its
form.
Ambiguity
and
depth
Literary
language
carries
multiple
meanings
simultaneously.
"To
be
or
not
to
be"
is
not
a
question
that
has
a
single
correct
answer.
The
ambiguity
is
the
point.
Richness
of
interpretation
is
a
sign
of
quality,
not
of
vagueness.
The
text
doesn't
tell
you
what
to
think;
it
gives
you
something
to
think
with.
Attention
to
form
Literature
is
conscious
of
how
it
says
things,
not
just
what.
The
shape
of
a
sonnet
(14
lines,
iambic
pentameter,
a
volta,
a
couplet)
creates
a
specific
pressure
that
shapes
meaning.
The
way
Faulkner's
sentences
sprawl
and
double
back
enacts
the
consciousness
they
describe.
Cultural
durability
Literary
works
accrue

through
time.
Hamlet
means
something
different
in
1601,
1900,
and
2025:
each

This
accretion
of
interpretation
distinguishes
literature
from
entertainment,
which
is
consumed
and
discarded.
The
canon
is,
in

a

of
works
that

this
test.

Poetry

The

The

/

line

to

Poetry
is

that

Extended

The

Its

Cervantes's

(1605)

Its

Drama

A

This

It

that

The

tense:

Every

Romanticism
reacted

Realism

Naturalism

Modernism

Postmodernism

Understanding

The

The

list
of

considered
essential
to

education,

most
contested

The
"canon"
(from

Greek
for

rod
or
standard)
is
not
a
fixed
list.
No
authoritative
body
issues
it.
It
is
a
collective
judgment,
accumulated
over
centuries
through
university
syllabi,
critical
editions,
translation

and
cultural
reproduction.
Harold
Bloom's

Canon
(1994)
named
26

F.R.
Leavis's

(1948)
named
4

The

argue

it
preserves

from
the

of
time.
Its
critics
(feminist,
postcolonial,
and
Marxist)
argue
that
it
reflects
the
preferences
of
dead
white
European
men
and
systematically
excludes
women,
non-Western
writers,
and
working-class
voices.
Both
arguments
are
substantially
correct,
which
makes
the
canon
question
one
of
the
most
productive
debates
in
contemporary
literary
culture.

Do

need
to
know
Greek
mythology
to
understand
Western
literature?
show
answer
Not
to
enjoy
it,
but
yes
to
understand
it
fully.
Greek
mythology
is
the
deep
grammar
of
Western
literary
allusion.
When
Keats
writes
"On
First
Looking
into
Chapman's
Homer,"
the
force
of
the
poem
depends
on
knowing
what
Homer
meant
to
the
ancient
world.
When
Joyce
names
his
novel
Ulysses,
the
entire
book
is
in
dialogue
with
Homer's
Odyssey.
When
a
character
is
described
as
Promethean,
Oedipal,
or
Narcissistic,
those
words
carry
myth
inside
them.
You
can
experience
the
surface
without
the
mythology;
the
depth
is
inaccessible
without
it.
A
few
weeks
with
a
good
mythology
handbook
(Graves,
Edith
Hamilton,
or
Ovid's
Metamorphoses
directly)
pays
compound
literary
interest
for
a
lifetime.

Are
myths
just
ancient
religious
belief,
or
do
they
have
something
to
say
to
modern
readers?
show
answer
Myths
are
not
failed
or
superseded
religion.
They
are
narrative
patterns
that
encode
persistent
aspects
of
human
experience.
The
Oedipus
myth
is
interesting
not
because
ancient
Greeks
believed
it
literally,
but
because
Freud
found
in
it
a
map
of
the
unconscious
that
still
generates
clinical
and
interpretive
work.
The
myth
of
Persephone
still
structures
how
we
think
about
seasons,
loss,
and
return.
The
story
of
Prometheus
still
describes
the
ambivalence
we
feel
about
technology
and
human
overreach.
Myths
survive
because
the
problems
they
encode
(how
to
live
with
mortality,
what
we
owe
each
other,
how
power
corrupts)
are
not
problems
that
get
solved.
They
get
re-staged
in
new
form
in
every
generation.
